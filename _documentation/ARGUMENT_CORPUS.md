# Morality, Between Us: argument corpus

> **Historical (editions 1–4).** Edition 5 (build 0.6.0) replaces the constitutive thesis with the claim that objective moral facts are not needed. See `ARGUMENT_STRUCTURE.md` and `EDITION5_REBUILD.md`.

The defended thesis is **constitutive**: human moral standards gain their validity through what can be justified to those affected under reciprocal, informed and unforced consideration of their claims. Interpersonal justification is proposed as part of what makes a standard valid, rather than merely a useful way to discover a separate moral truth. Individuals interpret, choose, dissent and remain answerable for their actions.

This is an authored philosophical argument. The historical and empirical sources constrain its factual premises and supply serious cases; they do not scientifically prove its metaethics. Runtime edition 4 contains 41 source entries, 12 rooms, 39 main response steps, 14 conditional continuations and three selectable concerns per room. The 53 authored steps contain 212 choices; a visitor sees only the branches activated by their current answers. The foundational 20-source access record is retained below; it is not the count of the expanded runtime or a claim that all sources have been read in full.

## Define the disputed claim first

“Objective” can refer to at least three different things in this inquiry:

- **Consequences independent of wishes:** an injury or atmospheric effect can occur whatever someone hopes or approves.
- **Publicly accountable judgment:** a claim can face shared evidence, impartial comparison and criticism beyond one person's current preference.
- **Validity independent of evaluative endorsement:** a moral judgment may be true without anyone's endorsement making it true.

The last is the central realist challenge. It does not mean that moral facts must exist in a world without sentient beings or must be unrelated to human needs. Railton explicitly develops a relational naturalist realism. Changing a person's needs or circumstances is not the same counterfactual as changing only the judgment that those needs matter. The exhibit must not defeat one meaning by substituting another. [Railton, *Moral Realism*](https://r.jordan.im/download/philosophy/railton1986.pdf), [Street, *A Darwinian Dilemma*](https://harjitbhogal.com/Flukes2024/Street%202006%20-%20A%20Darwinian%20dilemma%20for%20realist%20theories%20of%20value.pdf).

“Intersubjective” here is therefore not a poll, a majority's decree, bargaining power, an individual's passing desire, or a claim that every historical morality already satisfied fair constraints. It is the proposed dependence of moral validity on defensible relations among affected perspectives. Actual unanimity is unnecessary. A solitary dissenter can identify a justified complaint before anyone else acknowledges it; infants and others unable to participate can have represented interests. Representation and the comparison of conflicting complaints remain substantive tasks.

## Good, bad and the evaluative standpoint

The opening contains a substantial **Good and bad** section: three exhibits and three main questions. It begins by separating instrumental success, benefit to someone and moral worth. A service can meet its chosen aim while its aim or distribution of burdens remains morally objectionable. A welfare claim needs a beneficiary, circumstances and a defensible account of interests; current approval can misidentify what benefits a person. Moral assessment asks which ends and burdens deserve support and what is owed. Geach's account of attributive evaluation challenges a single context-free use of “good,” but he also rejects reducing its descriptive force to the speaker's commendation. His argument does not establish individual subjectivism. [Geach, paragraphs 1–3 and 8–12](https://fair-use.org/peter-t-geach/good-and-evil).

“Subjective” can mean that a subject makes the judgment from a standpoint. It can instead mean that the judgment's truth is fixed by that person's approval. The first does not entail the second; all judgments have judges. Moore treats the nature of goodness as a substantive philosophical matter that cannot be settled by verbal definition. Railton's realist account provides an especially relevant countermodel: human interests and relationships can ground assessment while present approval is mistaken. These rivals make the semantic shortcut untenable without settling the broader dispute. [Moore, chapter I §§5–10](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/53430/53430-h/53430-h.htm), [Railton](https://r.jordan.im/download/philosophy/railton1986.pdf).

The positive intersubjective argument adds a premise openly. When an evaluation claims authority over another life, that person's comparable interests deserve standing in its justification. Private satisfaction does not by itself answer the serious burden imposed on someone else. The proposal places interpersonal validity in a defensible relation among those affected, constrained by evidence, agency and criticism. This makes human evaluation more demanding than preference without pretending that the word “good” proves the account. Its standing and sufficiency premises remain defended philosophical commitments.

Hume provides a precedent for this move beyond private advantage: his sentimental account distinguishes moral approval from immediate self-interest and describes correction toward a steadier general standpoint. MORARG does not adopt every part of his psychology or claim his account proves its own moral foundation. [T 3.1.2.1–4](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/3/1/2), [T 3.3.1.15–18](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/3/3/1).

Certainty concerns the judge's conviction. By itself it cannot establish truth, stance-independence or falsity. Emotion may direct attention to a neglected burden; calmness is not a substitute for reasons. The final opening exercise therefore asks how a confident judgment should face a reasoned objection. Hearing the objection, explaining a principle, examining emotional experience and suspending judgment receive specific feedback without a philosophical winner. The twelve new choices add no inference flags.

## The positive argument

The map in `ARGUMENT_MAP.json` distinguishes the descriptive background, normative starting points, relational explanation and disputed grounding step. The complete numbered defence and its hard tests are in [AUTHORITY_ARGUMENT.md](AUTHORITY_ARGUMENT.md).

1. **The subject matter is a shared life.** Human actions and institutions alter other lives, options and relationships. Those effects supply something beyond the decision-maker's preference to examine. How we acquire moral reactions is relevant to explanation, but not sufficient for justification.
2. **Standing is a moral premise we defend.** A person's comparable interest should not be discounted merely because it belongs to someone else. When someone demands protection for their own agency while denying a comparable claim, they owe a relevant difference. Particular responsibilities and differing needs can justify distinctions; bare power does not answer the complaint within this account. The argument is an appeal to consistency, recognition and the legitimacy of claims over others, not a deduction of universal equality from self-interest.
3. **Reciprocity specifies a relation of authority.** A demand presented as morally governing shared conduct must be answerable to the people who bear its costs. A threat may secure compliance; a private preference may motivate its author. Neither alone supplies that reciprocal relation. Mutual protection is one practical reason for sustaining it, but recognition of agency and protection of the powerless do work that prudential bargaining cannot.
4. **Justification has real constraints.** Identify affected interests, use accurate evidence, compare feasible alternatives, allow unforced criticism, examine unequal burdens, and require reasons for exceptions. Actual endorsement under coercion or ignorance is insufficient. Revisability is substantive: specify evidence or an objection that could change a conclusion. A person cannot erase another's complaint simply by refusing to hear it.
5. **Take the constitutive step openly.** Our proposal is that meeting those justified demands constitutes moral validity for human interpersonal conduct. The quality of the justification is its basis, rather than only a symptom of an additional independent moral property. This explains how the standard can constrain present preference, condemn accepted abuse and improve through criticism while remaining grounded in human lives.
6. **Prefer the integrated account with the decisive premise exposed.** It connects what is owed, who can demand it, who is owed repair, and how a newly understood burden corrects an accepted rule. Legitimate agency can also determine an obligation when several arrangements are permissible. These features give the account positive explanatory fit. A strong relational realist can preserve them while grounding authority in independent normative truths. The preference for the relation as sufficient ground is therefore a defended normative-explanatory commitment; the present cases do not establish comparative superiority or the impossibility of an independent explanation.

Steps 2–4 are defended normative commitments. Step 5 is the disputed constitutive proposal; step 6 explains its positive warrant without claiming an established abductive victory. None is silently inferred from cooperation, agreement, animal behaviour or a policy's measured success. Local construction may coexist with realism about its starting reasons; the broader antirealist ambition remains unproved. The account draws on interpersonal justification without attributing that broader extension to Scanlon. [Scanlon's primary essay](https://sites.pitt.edu/~mthompso/readings/scanlon_contractualism.pdf), [his later reasons project](https://academic.oup.com/book/6445), and [Street's distinct constructivist project](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1747-9991.2009.00280.x) are comparison sources, not interchangeable doctrines.

This is a proposal about human interpersonal morality, not a derivation of all normative facts or a complete theory of every animal, future-person and political-authority question. It may leave more than one defensible option. A fair decision procedure can permit action without claiming that every losing position has become false.

The sufficiency argument now receives two direct tests. First, hold every practical reason and verdict fixed and compare what grounds their authority: justified relations, independent normative truths, a hybrid or an unresolved question. The realist need not produce an extra first-order complaint. Second, test the conditions against a serious bodily burden imposed for many trivial conveniences. Formal inclusion does not settle the weighting. A credible wrong surviving the stated conditions must be able to force revision rather than be automatically renamed a hidden procedural defect. Construction of a standard and discovery of what it requires can coexist. [Primary review](PRIMARY_REVIEW.md).

## Authority, representation and conflicting claims

Calling a norm constitutive of agency, or a practice inescapable, does not by itself establish a reason to comply with it. Enoch's primary argument presses exactly this gap. MORARG instead defends standing and reciprocity through the claims an interpersonal demand must answer. It preserves the victim's complaint against an unmoved aggressor while acknowledging that universal categorical authority has not been derived from premises everyone accepts. [Enoch's primary text](https://www.scribd.com/document/809394411/week05-Enoch2006).

Scanlon supplies a particularly close rival: his 2009 lecture calls his contractualist account of right and wrong constructive while retaining a prior understanding of reasons. The same lecture distinguishes justifying our beliefs about reasons from what makes those reasons obtain. This prevents the exhibit from treating reflective equilibrium as an automatic moral truthmaker. Wide scrutiny can improve warrant without a neutral starting point; coherence alone cannot distinguish a well-organised hierarchy from a defensible equal-standing account. [Lecture 4, pp. 16–20](https://media.philosophy.ox.ac.uk/assets/pdf_file/0010/25858/Lecture_4.pdf).

Representation is made concrete: support the person's own communication, distinguish known wishes from welfare and prospective interests, disclose proxy conflicts, allow independent challenge and revisit the decision if the person can answer. These safeguards are proposed applications of agency and protection, not fictional consent. The future-person branch separately asks whether present reciprocal standing requires present participation; welfare-based duties remain a distinct option.

Conflicting claims require comparison of feasible alternatives, including delay, severity, likelihood, reversibility, agency and special responsibilities. The exhibit defends a presumption against sacrificing grave interests for minor convenience while declining to manufacture a complete metric. Equal standing does not imply identical treatment, an unrestricted individual veto, or automatic aggregation. The minority-service example now gives the majority a contested capacity/contribution argument and asks whether its relevance survives scrutiny; it no longer grants the dissenter victory in the premise.

## Why the killing/preference conflation fails

The specified example is deliberate killing of a non-threatening person for convenience, excluding self-defence and disputed end-of-life cases. The difference from a private taste concerns loss of a future, agency, dependence, imposed risk and the impossibility of refusal after the fact. The author adds the moral premise that those interests count and cannot be dismissed solely because they are another's.

A constructivist can consequently condemn the act through a demanding standard that does not depend on the aggressor's current approval. An expressivist gives a different account of condemnation. An error theorist can deny moral properties while opposing the act for retained nonmoral reasons. Error theory does not establish that every act is morally permissible; it challenges such positive moral classifications too. Antirealism is not amoralism, and none of these positions entails indifference as a personality trait. [Joyce, “Error Theory”](https://www.richard-joyce.com/s/joyce_2024_errortheory.pdf).

The aggressor who understands the complaint and does not care exposes a genuine authority problem. Failure to motivate is not automatically failure to give a reason; neither does addressing someone automatically establish a categorical obligation they must recognise. The other people still have their own reasons to consider protection and resistance. This leaves the deepest dispute visible without making the practical distinction between killing and flavour disappear.

## Realism must remain recognisable

A naturalist may identify value with facts about interests and circumstances and understand justification as discovering those facts. Railton's selected primary passages expressly make room for error in current moralities, relational objectivity and contextual variation. A different approach affirms irreducible normative truths: Scanlon's qualified normative cognitivism and Enoch's explicit robust realism require separate treatment. The latter's deliberative-indispensability argument is a serious positive claim, not an appeal to supernatural moral particles. Full-book access remains limited. [Railton](https://r.jordan.im/download/philosophy/railton1986.pdf), [Scanlon](https://academic.oup.com/book/6445), [Enoch](https://academic.oup.com/book/26213).

The intersubjective reply is that being answerable to constraints need not mean discovering a stance-independent truth. A constituted standard can be difficult to discern and can overrule present endorsement. The realist then asks why these constraints constitute authority rather than track it. That dispute is the proper point of pressure. Joyce's primary critique helps articulate this reply but does not settle the debate. [Joyce, “Taking Moral Skepticism Seriously”](https://www.richard-joyce.com/s/joyce_2014_symposiumenoch.pdf).

Street's evolutionary argument is an epistemic challenge with further empirical and philosophical premises. It is not “evolved, therefore false”. Her own taxonomy of realism is narrower in places than Railton's self-description; in particular she treats his account of individual nonmoral good differently. Do not turn this taxonomic difference into an unannounced refutation. Her conditional evolutionary setup and naturalist objection have now been inspected in the full primary article. [Street](https://harjitbhogal.com/Flukes2024/Street%202006%20-%20A%20Darwinian%20dilemma%20for%20realist%20theories%20of%20value.pdf).

## Evolved fairness: a deliberately fenced section

The dedicated room follows the opening distinction about objectivity. Its sequence is **behaviour → interpretation → normative authority**. The original capuchin result, a 2024 pooled analysis, 2025 capuchin and bonobo studies and two 2026 studies form a targeted comparison, not a systematic review of the entire psychology of justice. The source access and design differences are recorded in `EVOLVED_FAIRNESS_RESEARCH.md`.

The 2024 pooled null concerns accept/reject paradigms, while later studies use different group and frequency conditions. Positive and null results must be interpreted within those designs. A refusal does not identify a human-like justice concept; a null refusal effect does not show that a disparity was never noticed. These studies do not establish an innate, universal or fixed human moral rule. [2024 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2024.1452), [2025 capuchin study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2025.123257), [2026 chimpanzee study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42156064/), [2026 intermittent-inequity study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42709475/).

Human developmental evidence is separately presented: a 2025 coordinated helper-choice replication found no above-chance preference in its tested infant task, while a 2026 looking-time study concerns expectations about particular caring relationships at a different age. These are different measures, not a direct positive-versus-negative replication pair. The published correction to the 2025 study is noted in the dedicated research record; it did not change the main conclusions. [ManyBabies study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39600132/), [relationship-expectation study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41961855/).

Affective and comparative capacities can be inputs into norm formation. They may draw attention to a burden; they do not validate every reaction. The preferred constitutive account therefore makes room for an evolved sense of fairness while requiring public reasons about standing, relevant differences and justified costs. Affective origin neither proves nor discredits moral truth. People reporting anger or distress retain a fair hearing; the feedback evaluates a stated claim or inference rather than emotion itself.

## Choices and authored assessments

Each room has three concerns to collect. The opening has six main response steps; the other eleven rooms have three each. Fourteen conditional continuations deepen foundation questions and all three cases, including the same-verdict grounding comparison and the authority-defeater test. Concerns express priorities, questions or tensions, never a truth score. Four choices per step allow substantive commitments, reflection and principled disagreement. The source IDs attached to each choice support the stated factual premise or philosophical context; the feedback is authored reasoning, not a claim that a source graded that visitor.

Only `inference` and `unsupported` trigger review. The current 13 flagged choices each name a missing inference or an overreaching factual claim; the complete audit is in `ASTRA_ARGUMENT_REVIEW.md` and `ARGUMENT_MAP.json`. Emotional reports, realism, error theory, uncertainty, personal loyalty, privacy, retributive desert and challenges to equal standing remain unflagged as such. Some flags target overclaims supporting the author's own thesis. Revision and continuing unchanged remain available.

Five separate checks compare literal current commitments: retained nonmoral reasons versus no reason against the same killing; a final identifiable-data veto versus continuing that same use; a fixed punitive ceiling versus an extra punitive year; a protected needs floor or funded-compensation requirement versus proceeding without it; and required present participation versus a present reciprocal claim for people unable to participate. The last is the narrow cross-room check. These rules do not compare philosophical identities or treat earlier-to-later changes of view as contradictions by themselves.

Every check states its scope and shows the selected claims. A changed-mind or distinction acknowledgement records an explanation; it does not make incompatible claims logically compatible or remove the visible pair. Revising an answer recomputes the check. Inactive and stale branch answers cannot supply a current conflict; a reactivated answer needs reconfirmation. The cases' new facts are stipulated, including research withdrawal terms, a punitive maximum and a household needs floor; they are not empirical results imported from the case sources.

### Metaethical surveys

#### `sep-metaethics`

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`

**Source:** Geoff Sayre-McCord (2007; revised 2023), [“Metaethics”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/metaethics/). **Kind:** scholarly survey. **Locator:** §§1–3, especially the distinctions among cognitivism, non-cognitivism, constructivism, and error theory. **Access:** full HTML available; selected sections and relevant passages reviewed, not an end-to-end reading. **Use:** frames metaethics as inquiry into the assumptions and commitments of moral thought, talk, and practice; makes clear that descriptive explanation and normative judgment are different jobs. **Does not establish:** which metaethical theory is correct or that the empirical fact of social practice gives morality authority.

#### `sep-realism`

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`

**Source:** Geoff Sayre-McCord (2005; revised 2026), [“Moral Realism”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-realism/). **Kind:** scholarly survey. **Locator:** §§1–3, including realism’s core, disagreement, and epistemic challenges. **Access:** full HTML available; selected sections reviewed, not an end-to-end reading. **Use:** defines the shared realist core as cognitivism plus at least some true moral claims and surveys variation over what makes them true. It shows why mind-independence and objectivity need careful definition. **Does not establish:** that realists must claim moral knowledge is easy, unanimous, or beyond ordinary evidence.

#### `sep-relativism`

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`

**Source:** Chris Gowans (2004; revised 2026), [“Moral Relativism”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-relativism/). **Kind:** scholarly survey. **Locator:** §§2, 4–6, and 8: forms and arguments, descriptive diversity, metaethical relativism, and tolerance. **Access:** full HTML available; selected sections reviewed, not an end-to-end reading. **Use:** separates descriptive diversity from the thesis that truth or justification is relative; distinguishes subjectivism from group-relativist views and separates tolerance from metaethics. **Does not establish:** that any one relativist view follows from empirical diversity, or that relativism entails tolerance.

#### `sep-constructivism`

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`

**Source:** Carla Bagnoli (2011; revised 2025), [“Constructivism in Metaethics”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/constructivism-metaethics/). **Kind:** scholarly survey. **Locator:** §§1–5 and §6 on objections, especially society-based, Kantian, Humean, and hybrid approaches. **Access:** full HTML available; selected sections reviewed, not an end-to-end reading. **Use:** treats constructivism as a family and explains how some versions aim to preserve objective standards while tying them to constrained practical reasoning. It notes that not every version is anti-realist and identifies arbitrariness, authority, and “unconstructed” constraints as live problems. **Does not establish:** that any social agreement or actual practice is itself a valid construction.

#### `sep-contractualism`

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`

**Source:** Elizabeth Ashford and Tim Mulgan (2007; revised 2018), [“Contractualism”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2023/entries/contractualism/). **Kind:** scholarly survey. **Locator:** §§1–4, with attention to the Scanlonian account and objections about rejection, aggregation, and demandingness. **Access:** full Winter 2023 archive available; selected sections reviewed, not an end-to-end reading. **Use:** distinguishes Scanlonian contractualism from Rawlsian contract views and from actual agreement; makes clear that reasonable rejection requires substantive interpretation. **Does not establish:** that a procedure of agreement is self-justifying or that every stakeholder can be represented without difficulty.

#### `sep-expressivism`

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`

**Source:** Mark van Roojen (2004; revised 2023), [“Moral Cognitivism vs. Non-Cognitivism”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-cognitivism/). **Kind:** scholarly survey. **Locator:** §2.4 on expressivism and §§3–4 on inference, truth-talk, and the embedding problem. **Access:** full HTML available; selected sections reviewed, not an end-to-end reading. **Use:** distinguishes cognitivist subjectivist theories from non-cognitivist theories and shows how expressivists understand moral sentences as expressing attitudes or commitments. **Does not establish:** that all moral language is mere emotion or that expressivism has no account of logic, truth-like discourse, or disagreement.

#### `sep-disagreement`

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`

**Source:** Folke Tersman (2021; revised 2026), [“Moral Disagreement”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/disagreement-moral/). **Kind:** scholarly survey. **Locator:** §§1, 3–6, on the nature of disagreement, arguments from disagreement, epistemic implications, and over-generalization. **Access:** full HTML available; selected sections reviewed, not an end-to-end reading. **Use:** presents disagreement as having several possible roles and explanations; flags empirical premises, safety/reliability assumptions, and the risk of extending skeptical arguments to other domains. **Does not establish:** that disagreement is irrelevant or that any participant is irrational; it surveys arguments whose force remains contested.

**Record:** `ts_ms=1793117092244`
