# Comparative fairness: evidence and limits

This note accompanies the new fairness room. It is a targeted literature assessment, not a systematic search or an independent reanalysis. Checked 27 October 2026 UTC. The question is whether observed responses to reward distributions help explain capacities from which human moral practices may develop. Observations of refusal are not direct measurements of justice concepts or moral authority.

## Primary studies

- [Brosnan and de Waal, *Monkeys reject unequal pay* (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01963): the original study reports capuchins refusing exchanges or lower-value rewards after a partner received a more attractive reward. The [primary article PDF](https://courses.washington.edu/anmind/Brosnan%20%26%20de%20Waal%20-%20monkeys%20reject%20unequal%20pay%20-%20Nature%202003.pdf) was indexed and selected abstract and method passages inspected. The authors interpreted this as evidence of an evolutionary origin of inequity aversion. Later work contests whether comparison with the partner or expectation of better food explains refusals. The original observation is not a demonstration that monkeys possess human concepts of justice.
- [Ritov, Völter, Raihani and Engelmann, *No evidence for inequity aversion in non-human animals* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2024.1452): an individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 23 dyadic accept/reject studies covering 302 subjects, 18 species and 60,430 observations. It did not find statistically clear support for the predicted inequity effect in the pooled analysis or preregistered species subsets; disappointment from seeing a better reward is a competing account. The [indexed primary full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11597404/) methods, results and discussion were inspected; direct opening encountered a browser challenge. The authors obtained data for 23 of 30 eligible studies, excluded group settings and other response paradigms, and note that social or environmental context may matter. A null effect is not proof of zero effect or no capacity to notice inequality.
- [Martínez and Brosnan, capuchin group context (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2025.123257): the [publisher abstract and indexed excerpts](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347225001848) report refusals of low-value rewards under disadvantageous inequality and also responses to contrast and advantageous inequality. The number of exchanges initiated depended on food value, not the partner's reward. The full paper and data were not independently reviewed. This more social setting yields a different result measure and context from the dyadic meta-analysis; it does not independently establish which mechanism explains every refusal.
- [Martínez et al., chimpanzee group context (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42156064/): the primary abstract reports a token-exchange study of 27 chimpanzees. Negative responses occurred when low-value food was received, and were stronger in the presence of closely bonded individuals; medium- and high-value food was rarely rejected. The full paper was not reviewed. Group relationships and food quality are relevant to interpretation, but the abstract does not establish an innate moral concept.
- [Simmons et al., intermittent inequity in four primate species (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42709475/): the [publisher abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ajp.70196) reports no increased refusals relative to controls when unequal trials occurred 25% or 50% of the time. The authors propose further work on frequencies and other response measures. Full text was not reviewed; absence of a refusal effect does not show that animals failed to notice differences.

## Interpretation for the exhibit

These results do not collapse into a simple verdict. Earlier findings describe observed refusals; the pooled dyadic evidence questions whether they consistently track another's better treatment beyond reward expectations. Later group studies report context-sensitive responses; the intermittent-trial study identifies another boundary. Group and dyadic designs, populations, measures and controls differ, so neither the 2024 analysis nor the newer experiments automatically cancels the other. Broader claims about evolved justice would need evidence beyond these tasks, including developmental and comparative research.

An evolved capacity may help people attend to outcomes and participate in shared norms. It cannot, by itself, establish which interests count, which distributions are justified, or why any proposed procedure is authoritative. Conversely, an evolutionary origin alone does not refute stance-independent moral truth; an epistemic challenge requires a further argument about reliability. The exhibit advances a constitutive account of moral standards as a philosophical argument, not an empirical conclusion forced by primate research.

## Human development sidebar: early social expectations

- [Lucca, Yuen, Wang and colleagues (ManyBabies), *Infants’ Social Evaluation of Helpers and Hinderers* (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39600132/), *Developmental Science* 28, e13581, DOI 10.1111/desc.13581. In a preregistered multi-lab replication, 1,018 infants were tested and 567 aged 5.5–10.5 months were included; 37 laboratories collected data, 34 contributed included data. In the social hill task 49.34% chose the helper, not distinguishable from chance. This tests manual choice in that paradigm, not every social capacity. The primary abstract and indexed participant details were inspected; no independent data reanalysis. The [published correction](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/desc.70029) replaces an exploratory habituation main-effect claim with a centered analysis finding no main effect; it states the study's conclusions are unchanged. The principal choice result above is not the corrected exploratory claim.
- [Pepe, Woo, Thomas and Powell, *Infants use helping to infer the existence and strength of caring relationships* (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41961855/), *PNAS* 123, e2531771123, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2531771123. Four preregistered looking-time experiments with infants aged 14–15 months support the authors' interpretation that helping informs expectations about particular social relationships and relative priorities; the initial expectation did not generalize to a new recipient. Primary abstract and selected indexed methods, results and discussion were inspected; no independent replication. Looking-time patterns are indirect measures and do not establish an explicit moral rule.

The studies use different ages, outcomes and designs: preference for a helper is not the same measure as expectations about a relationship. The 2026 study is not a direct replication of the 2025 null finding. Together they illustrate possible developmental routes to social understanding, while neither establishes an innate, universal sense of justice, normative judgment, or the authority of a moral principle.

## Pass003: deeper empirical checks

The positive [Radovanović et al. bonobo paper (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12001075/) adds a direct challenge to a single disappointment explanation. Selected primary methods, results and limitations were inspected. Six bonobos from one captive group entered the new work; a token-exchange analysis also pooled an older sample. Human/machine delivery and partner presence were manipulated in the second study. The interaction, rather than a uniform partner effect, matters: the authors describe only partial consistency with inequity aversion. Variation in partners, small samples and constrained responses limit generalisation. This warrants taking social comparison seriously without treating an animal response as a moral concept or a universal norm. No independent reanalysis occurred.

Selected methods, results and discussion of [Martínez et al. (2026)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/405041409_Chimpanzees%27_responses_to_inequity_in_a_group_context_are_influenced_by_food_quality_and_their_relationships_with_the_group_members_present) are now available in the publisher-provided primary paper, superseding the earlier abstract-only limit. Six captive groups participated voluntarily; each of the two studies analysed 27 participants, not necessarily identical membership. Equity and contrast conditions help separate mechanisms. Advantageous refusals were too rare for statistical analysis. Social affiliation was not randomly assigned; its observed direction opposed the prediction, and the authors discuss attention, rank and competitive explanations. The paper strengthens the importance of context while leaving mechanism and generalisation open. The later paper is a different design from the pooled accept/reject analysis, not an automatic refutation of it. No independent data analysis occurred.

The 2025 capuchin and September 2026 intermittent-inequity papers retain their stated abstract/indexed-access limits. The full paper endpoint for the former returned an access error; the latter publisher endpoint exposed the abstract rather than full methods. A bibliographic lead is not a completed methods review.

For the September 2026 study, the [publisher’s data-availability statement](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ajp.70196) points to an author GitHub repository. Opening that exact linked repository returned HTTP 404 during pass003. This records a failed retrieval, not a conclusion that the data never existed or cannot be obtained. No reanalysis was performed.
