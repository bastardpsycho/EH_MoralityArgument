# Visitor pressure tests for the morality argument

This is an independent qualitative review of the pass001 content and a set of acceptance checks for the current argument pass. It is not a user study or evidence that visitors actually responded in these ways.

## First impressions

The original sequence distinguishes many serious positions but feels like a philosophy examination: two questions per room often contrast a defensible answer with a visibly exaggerated error. A visitor can pass by spotting absolutes without examining the proposed position. Abstract terms such as “normative bridge”, “stance-independent” and “reasonable rejection” need a plain-language example before their technical name. “Intersubjective” should be introduced as a proposal about reasons and relationships, not as a claim that people merely vote on morality.

Before asking a visitor to assess objectivity, distinguish at least: independent of my current approval; impartial between people; and independent of all minds. Give the visitor a sentence they can challenge: “A person's pain, terror and loss of future are real even if people disagree about what gives them moral weight. Rejecting mind-independent moral facts does not mean killing is harmless or that condemnation is merely a food preference.” The hard question then remains: what makes those facts reasons that another person ought to respect, especially if they reject the proposed standard? This avoids treating a controversial metaethical position as either a license to kill or a proof of the exhibit's conclusion.

## Critic's questions and what a satisfactory response needs

- **Why listen to someone else?** “We depend on one another” alone supports prudence, not necessarily fairness to someone unable to benefit or punish us. A stronger case starts from a person claiming protection, freedom, or authority over others: ask what distinguishes their comparable interests from those they would discount. State equal consideration and non-domination as moral commitments, explain why their asymmetrical denial is hard to justify, and acknowledge that this does not logically compel an agent who rejects the demand for justification itself.
- **Does a lone reformer lose against the group?** Show an accepted exclusion, the minority person's specific experience of its costs, and a majority's rationale. The visitor should be able to say the reformer is right even if every powerful participant objects. A critical standard must constrain actual consensus; name the standard and why it is proposed rather than assuming the majority eventually agrees.
- **Who cannot speak?** Children, people with severe communication impairments, future generations and animals can have interests without bargaining or consenting. Identify their possible interests, designate contestable representation, and say representatives may be mistaken or have conflicting interests. A speech-based procedure alone cannot establish their standing.
- **Can lived experience be evidence?** Describe a concrete encounter with humiliation or fear as firsthand evidence of impacts, while allowing further inquiry about causes and scope. Avoid requiring a composed or detached presentation before a person's account can count. Recognise that opposing participants may also bear real costs.
- **Is this still objective?** A constructivist may endorse impartial or procedure-dependent objectivity, and a realist can endorse public justification. The exhibit should argue for its precise claim rather than presenting all objectivity as one rival.
- **Must I accept a false binary?** Let a visitor support strong reasons against violence while doubting mind-independent facts, remain realist while supporting dialogue, or reject the author's procedure without embracing arbitrary preference. In each case report the position selected, not a substituted conclusion.

## Scenario tests

1. **Confidence and safety:** A friend discloses a credible plan to seriously harm someone and asks you to keep it secret. Telling a trusted person can prevent harm but breaks confidence and may expose the friend to intervention. Keeping confidence preserves trust but leaves someone at risk. Invite a choice about an appropriately limited disclosure, hear the reasons for alternatives, and avoid implying that trust is valueless when safety is prioritised. Facts about imminence, available help and the friend's agency may change the answer.
2. **Reform and exclusion:** A community's public meeting rules exclude residents who cannot attend in person. A disabled resident describes missed decisions and asks for remote participation; organisers worry about cost, privacy and public access. An accessible alternative has a cost, while the existing rule places repeated burdens on a minority. Neither majority approval nor a single person's request settles all implementation details; the case makes the substantive commitment to equal standing testable.
3. **An absent future person:** A town can fund a near-term service now or protect a resource for future residents. Give plausible benefits, uncertainty, and genuine present need, rather than making one option costless. Ask who represents the future, who bears today's cost, and how to revise when evidence changes.

The cases are illustrative thought experiments, not empirical findings. Distinguish a room-specific value choice from a later full case study: if a room asks the visitor to pick two or three values, the wording and feedback must track the actual picks and their tension, not infer an action, belief, rank ordering, or consent they never supplied.

## Practical acceptance checks

- Every room presents the requested two or three value selections in a way the visitor can understand, revise and review; constraints should be stated before selection.
- For each selectable value or decision, trace the displayed explanation and private record. Check that the wording names the visitor's actual selection, preserves any competing concern, and does not call principled opposition an inference error. Check partial and revised selections as well as the happy path.
- Test a skeptical path throughout: reject or question reciprocity, retain realism, choose a minority perspective, care about a non-speaking being, and leave unresolved; the experience should accurately describe those choices.
- Ask a non-specialist to explain in their own words the exhibit's thesis, which sense of objectivity it challenges, why it treats killing as serious, and what remains disputed. This is a proposed test, not a result.
- Check that factual and normative claims are distinguishable, source links support what they are attached to, and illustrative cases are not presented as research evidence.

## Pass002 revised-data read

The rewritten argument addresses the major pass001 concerns: it distinguishes senses of objectivity before the killing example; separates denial of stance-independent facts from indifference; gives reciprocity positive reasons and identifies the authority gap; makes dissent effective against unanimous approval; and includes the interests of those unable to negotiate. The confidentiality and research choices preserve costs on both sides. The inspected choices and explanations preserve serious realist and skeptical positions and match the selected rationale. This is an authored-text review, not a completed visitor study or an exhaustive browser and export test.

Two small wording improvements were sent to the argument lead: qualify that penicillin was withheld after it became a standard treatment, and replace “Agreed scope limit” with wording that acknowledges a challenge without presuming the visitor's full view. The thesis's “best practised” is a stronger comparative claim than the exhibit establishes conclusively; a defensible-way formulation would match its acknowledged limits, though an explicitly defended preference is also possible.
