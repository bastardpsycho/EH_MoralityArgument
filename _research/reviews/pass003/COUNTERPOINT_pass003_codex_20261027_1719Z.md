## Finding

The stronger thesis is now stated plainly: human moral standards are constituted by what can be justified to those affected under reciprocal, informed and unforced consideration; actual approval does not make a standard valid. The exhibit has a defensible route to that claim, and it acknowledges that its starting commitments are substantive. Its case studies can show what the proposed account helps people do. They cannot, by themselves, show that adequate justification constitutes validity rather than tracks a prior truth.

The strongest remaining objection is not “people disagree.” It is the authority and dependence problem: what makes the account’s standards of standing, relevance, reasonableness and weight authoritative, and can the account explain that without presupposing reasons it says are constituted by the procedure? A realist may accept every case judgment and safeguard while saying the procedure discovers or tracks truths about interests and reasons. The argument should continue to defend the constitutive view; it should not present agreement in the cases as evidence that the realist explanation has been ruled out.

## Strongest realist challenge

The sharpest primary-source challenge comes from T. M. Scanlon’s own contractualist account. In his 2009 Locke Lecture 4, Scanlon describes contractualism about right and wrong as constructivist in a broad sense: wrongness depends on the result of a procedure comparing reasons individuals have to object to principles, whether or not anyone actually carries the procedure out. He then says that this account depends on a prior understanding of reasons. His question is whether reasons themselves can also be constructed; he argues that some underived truths about reasons remain and require explanation. This does **not** refute contractualism about interpersonal wrongness. It does show that a procedure can constitute one moral domain while relying on a more basic normative account. MORARG therefore needs to argue why its own premises—equal standing, agency, interests, relevant differences and limits on imposed harm—are constitutive conditions of human moral validity rather than truths or reasons on which the construction depends. See [Scanlon, *Being Realistic about Reasons*, Lecture 4](https://media.philosophy.ox.ac.uk/assets/pdf_file/0010/25858/Lecture_4.pdf), especially the discussion of contractualism and the prior account of reasons.

David Enoch supplies a second pressure test. In [“Agency, Shmagency”](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID2607208_code357518.pdf?abstractid=2607208&mirid=1), he argues that standards constitutive of an activity do not alone give someone a reason to take part in that activity: a person can ask why to be an agent, rather than a “shmagent,” or why to participate in the relevant practice. This is not a direct refutation of MORARG, whose claim is interpersonal and about affected lives rather than merely the constitutive aims of agency. It creates a fair question for the authority room: if a powerful person rejects reciprocal justification, is the framework claiming that the person still has a reason, or only that other affected people may resist and protect themselves? The exhibit already distinguishes a reason’s authority from its motivational force; it should state what its constitutive account can claim about the refuser and what it leaves as a defended premise. Enoch’s [positive argument from deliberative indispensability](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID2607202_code357518.pdf?abstractid=2607202&mirid=1) also deserves a fair presentation: deliberation may seem to treat reasons as answerable to correctness, not authored by the deliberator. The argument needs a further premise to move from that apparent indispensability to stance-independent truth, but MORARG should answer the pressure rather than treating it as confusion.

A naturalist realist is a compatible, concrete rival. Railton’s [“Moral Realism”](https://r.jordan.im/download/philosophy/railton1986.pdf) develops a naturalistic account of objective interests using what an informed and idealized version of a person would want. On this view, the truthmaker can concern sentient lives, needs and relationships; it need not be independent of the existence of minds. Nor must it be made true by anyone’s current endorsement. Public scrutiny, reciprocal reasoning and personal judgment can be good ways to learn about those facts while the facts themselves explain why a claim is justified. MORARG’s proposed account has a different explanation, but the same evidence and outcome do not choose between them.

This also calls for careful use of Scanlon as a source. Scanlon’s contractualism offers a close model for comparing complaints and asking what principles people could reasonably reject. It does not establish the stronger MORARG metaethical thesis. His 2009 lecture and [2014 book description](https://academic.oup.com/book/6445) explicitly defend irreducibly normative truths about reasons and question whether a plausible general constructivist account of reasons can be given. Cite him for contractualist structure and interpersonal justification, while marking the disagreement about the status of reasons.

## Authority, circularity and representation tests

1. **The dominant-group test.** Give an association an orderly, informed, formally equal procedure. Its members exclude a minority from a service, cite a reason they regard as relevant, and the minority reports a serious burden. Ask what determines whether the reason is relevant and the burden decisive. If the answer is “the affected person’s complaint,” explain how it can be rejected when another affected party has a competing claim. If the answer invokes equal standing, dignity or harm, identify whether that is a constitutive starting point or a truth the procedure discovers. This tests whether the rule can criticize a coherent practice without simply defining “reasonable” as “the answer MORARG favors.”

2. **The unrepresented-person test.** A person who will bear the cost cannot participate or correct a proxy—for example, a child lacking capacity, a future person or a nonhuman animal. Who selects the representative; what evidence distinguishes the represented person’s interests from the proxy’s preferences; how can a mistaken proxy be challenged; and how should two proxies with incompatible accounts be adjudicated? Representation extends standing beyond bargaining power, but it is not yet a self-validating source of the represented person’s authority. The further standard must remain visible.

3. **The refusal test.** A powerful agent understands the reasons offered and still rejects reciprocal justification. The proposed account can say that the affected people have grounds to object and to protect themselves. To claim more—that the refuser still has a moral reason—MORARG must say whether that follows from the procedure’s constitutive status or from an independent premise about reasons. Do not let “does not motivate” be treated as “does not exist,” and do not let the refuser’s noncompliance count as a veto.

4. **The multiple-valid-outcomes test.** Consider a genuine conflict between a person’s bodily agency, another’s safety, and a third party’s severe burden. If two principles survive the stated tests, does the account say both are permissible, require a priority rule, or leave the decision indeterminate? A framework may be valuable without guaranteeing a unique answer. But it should make clear when unresolved pluralism is an intended result rather than an accidental gap.

These are pressure tests, not demands that MORARG derive every normative commitment from nonmoral facts. A constructivist can openly take substantive starting points. The burden is to defend why they are the right starting points for constituting validity, how objections to them can succeed, and what follows for dissenters and absent parties.

## Case-study audit

- **`reform-dissenter`:** The current context says the complaint identifies substantial burdens “without a relevant justification for the distinction.” That pre-grants the pivotal evaluation and makes the dissenter’s vindication easy. Keep it as an illustrative application if useful, but add a harder version where the majority offers a contested justification and the minority disputes its relevance. Ask how to decide between the claims before the scenario tells the visitor there is no relevant reason.
- **The killing case:** Killing a non-threatening person for convenience is an apt example of ordinary moral condemnation and of the distinction between antirealism and amoralism. It is intentionally an easy shared-outcome case, so it does not discriminate a naturalist realist explanation from a constitutive one. Keep it as a test of whether rejecting one foundation entails “no reasons”; do not count the common condemnation as evidence for the constitutive thesis.
- **Tuskegee, Norway and ozone:** Their factual limits are now more visible. Historical abuse, a bounded causal estimate and successful treaty coordination can constrain premises and show the practical value of public standards. The records do not establish the moral truthmaker. The ozone example in particular shows institutional coordination in response to physical facts; it does not independently validate burden shares.
- **Evolved fairness:** The dedicated room correctly separates observable refusals, competing explanations, social norms and moral authority. Mixed primate results can inform hypotheses about capacities. They establish neither a universal innate content of justice nor stance-independent authority; an evolutionary origin also does not refute realism. This is a useful boundary-setting room, not evidence that the intersubjective account is true.

The current choice set offers very few strict cross-step contradictions. Do not manufacture six by treating theory labels as mutually exclusive. The one strong same-scope check is:

## Concrete prompt replacements

**Replace the loaded `reform-dissenter` context with:**

> A common service is denied to a minority. The group cites a distinction it says is relevant; the minority describes a substantial burden and disputes that the distinction justifies it. What would decide whether the exclusion is justified?

Then offer genuinely different routes: compare the affected parties’ claims under defended constraints; ask whether the distinction tracks an independent moral truth; inspect whether the process lets the minority contest the facts and standard; or withhold judgment pending a stated fact. Avoid telling the visitor that no relevant justification exists before asking them to assess it.

**Strengthen the authority/circularity question with:**

> Assume a decision process is informed, uncoerced and open to every affected party or an accountable representative. Why do its constraints govern someone who rejects them?

Possible answers should distinguish: “they constitute the standards of mutual justification, even if someone refuses”; “they track objective truths about interests and reasons”; “they bind only within commitments I can defend”; and “I do not yet know.” Explain that the constitutive view is a defended thesis, not an output of the case facts.

**Make representation concrete with:**

> A person who will bear a serious burden cannot speak, and two proxies disagree about their interests. What makes either proxy accountable, and what standard should decide between them?

This forces the account to state how evidence, affected interests, agency and competing representations work together without implying that the current group’s approval settles the answer.
