# Independent authority critique (initial)

Review time: `ts_ms=1793125016000`. Scope: read-only critique of `AUTHORITY_ARGUMENT.md`, the current hub/climate/commons/hall/charter visitor text, `PRIMARY_REVIEW.md`, and primary-source support. No application files changed.

## What the argument now earns

The account makes meaningful progress by separating a relational explanandum (why a person can claim treatment from another and seek an answer or repair) from the further claim about what makes such a claim valid. It openly owns equal standing as a moral commitment, accepts that the conditions are incomplete, offers counterexamples that could defeat sufficiency, and states a realist countermodel that accepts the same practical reasons, relationships and correction. Its restriction to local interpersonal construction is warranted: this does not refute realism about prior reasons or all evaluative truth.

## Remaining gap: relational fit is not yet comparative ground

Premise 1, supported by Darwall, identifies a real distinction between an agent-neutral reason to improve a state and a second-personal claim whose acceptance involves authority and accountability. Darwall's 2009 text explicitly says authority, valid claim, responsibility-to-someone and second-personal reason are interdefinable, and that no proposition lacking those concepts entails them (pp. 1–2). That is excellent support for the kind of thing interpersonal obligation is; it is not evidence that reciprocal justification constitutes the validity of the claim. If used as a ground argument, the interdefinition risks moving the question into the term “valid authority.”

Premise 4's “unification” is a plausible explanatory fit: the same relation connects who may demand what, why a complaint remains live, and what repair/correction requires. But the realist can accept all those relational facts and say independent normative facts explain why this relation confers valid authority. Railton explicitly allows objective-but-relational moral properties, error in current morality, and moral applicability without motivation (1986, pp. 164–165). So the draft's relational realist is not a remote possibility; it is a close countermodel. “Locates authority in the relation” identifies the proposal's ground, but does not yet show that it explains validity better than the realist's relation-plus-prior-reasons account.

Do not state that the realist must add a new first-order complaint or practical verdict: the draft correctly withdraws that shortcut. The remaining dispute is explanatory priority. To make an abductive preference substantive, compare explanatory virtues symmetrically. Identify one feature of interpersonal validity the relational account explains better—if one can be shown—or state that the evidence leaves the grounding choice open and that the intersubjective foundation is a defended interpretive commitment. Darwall's second-personal analysis may establish that a realist theory must account for relational authority; it does not establish that those relations are the truthmaker.

## Adequacy and non-stipulative tests

The listed adequacy conditions are valuable and concrete. They also rely on normative judgments about who counts as materially affected, what qualifies as comparable, which distinctions are relevant, how severity and agency weigh, and what makes a response adequate. The draft acknowledges this instead of claiming a neutral procedure. It should make clear that these judgments are independently contestable inputs, not outputs of the procedure; a realist may ground them in prior reasons, while the constructivist defends their reciprocal role.

The failure condition—“a credible wrong that survives every test” challenges sufficiency—is a good safeguard against immunity-by-definition, but it needs one visitor-facing example. For instance, hold all stated facts, representation and noncoercion conditions fixed and ask whether an informed adult may authorize a permanent arrangement that removes future challenge rights for a large public benefit. If the answer is no, name and defend the agency/standing limit instead of relabeling the process inadequate. If yes, state what makes that exercise of present agency valid. Either result remains a moral argument rather than a mechanical test.

A coherent hierarchy that rejects equal standing should not be called inconsistent merely because it rejects the premise. The current text handles this fairly: ask whether it can give a relevant, position-exchangeable reason for the burden, then admit that the dispute is substantive. Likewise, if two standards survive, permissibility pluralism is different from contradictory duties; the draft's test is appropriately framed.

## Primary-source limits

- Darwall, “Authority and Second-Personal Reasons for Acting,” author-hosted Yale manuscript, selected pp. 1–7 and 13–16. The manuscript distinguishes second-personal from outcome-directed reasons and emphasizes conceptual interdependence; it does not prove equal standing or reciprocal-constructivist truthmaking.
- Forst, “The Justification of Basic Rights,” Berkeley-hosted 2016 article, selected §§1–2. It supplies a substantial positive model of equal normative authority and reciprocal justification, but expressly limits itself to recognized members of a legal-political order and grounds those rights in a basic moral right to justification (pp. 1–4). That is a substantive starting point and a legal-political scope, not a premise-free derivation or proof of MORARG's broader interpersonal metaethics.
- Railton, “Moral Realism,” *The Philosophical Review* 95 (1986), selected pp. 163–165. He explicitly characterizes his view as objective though relational, permits current moralities to be wrong, and allows moral requirements to apply even to an agent who lacks a reason to obey. This directly blocks treating relation, fallibility, or an unmoved refuser as constructivist-exclusive evidence.
