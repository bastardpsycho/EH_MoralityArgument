## Argument findings

### A-13: partly accepted

**What I accept.** Edition 5 said "every job", then "never rely on", then concluded "nothing depends on knowing whether". Those are different claims, and the copy slid between them. A run of "covered" answers across different kinds of job is not one proposition confirmed ten times. A realist can accept every reason and safeguard the exhibit offers. And Williams and Korsgaard are rival routes, not pieces of one account.

**What I did:**

- **One scope, stated once and carried through.** It appears in `brief` ("What it claims, exactly"), the thesis, the room takeaways and the ledger outcome: in *explaining* judgments, *justifying* decisions to one another, *protecting* people and *making sense of* our reasoning, we never need to *rely* on objective moral facts. Existence is not denied.
- **The realist-contractualist countermodel is stated in the opening, in your terms.** A realist can accept the same reasons and safeguards and say they are correct because they track objective truths.
- **The argument against that extra layer is given, and named for what it is.** If nothing in practice depends on it, it does no work. That is a parsimony argument, not a proof, and the copy no longer describes realism as an extra causal object.
- **Jobs carry a kind** (`jobKinds`: explaining, justifying, protecting, reasoning). The ledger shows it, so "covered" answers of one kind are not read as independent confirmations.
- Williams and Korsgaard are presented as rivals (room 07).

**Where I push back.** You recommend epistemic/practical independence as the thesis. As you note, a realist already accepts that. As the exhibit's thesis it would make the exhibit uncontroversial. Visitors would agree with it whatever they think about objectivity, and the ledger would test nothing. The Operator chose dispensability. Protocol rule 5 makes that the Operator's call, and it is a respectable position in the literature: Harman's explanatory challenge, Street, and Blackburn's quasi-realist economy are all in that family.

Your real objection is that dispensability has an argument gap, not that it is illegitimate. I agree there is a gap. The exhibit now states the premise that bridges it (the parsimony step) and lets the visitor reject it at the ledger. A visitor who thinks the realist's extra layer does work in some job answers "needs" there, and the outcome reports that the claim fails there.

I also accept your point about finite cases: the conclusion is now the visitor's judgment about these jobs, not a universal result.

**The is–ought room supports the scope rather than overreaching.** It argues that the gap rules out *naturalistic* routes to objective morality and leaves only non-natural facts. It flags "needs a mind, therefore not objective" as a step that doesn't follow (`gap-mind/follows`). Only then does it hand over to the dispensability question.
